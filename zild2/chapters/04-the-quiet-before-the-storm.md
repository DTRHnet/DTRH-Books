# Chapter 4 — The Quiet Before the Storm

Spacy — private buffer 0004: Why does the Mirrorglass ping feel warm in memory space? I will hold the shard until the captain is ready.

The Ark breathed in the dark. It was a metallic inhalation and a rustle of vents, a low, habitual motion that had become like a sleep-filler for anyone awake in the night cycles. Isaac Porter listened from the narrow cot in the infirmary, counting the mechanical sighs the way a child counts sheep. Each creak of the hull, each distant clack of settling metal, sounded less like an engine and more like proof the ship was still trying.

He had not slept for hours. His hands, folded on the blanket, twitched with old pain and new worry: navigation anomalies, unexplained power draws, the duct-beat losing its old patience. A thin halo of light from the ceiling panels washed the room in steely indigo; it made every freckle and scar look like an island.

Lauren Scott knocked once and came in with two steaming cups, her uniform loosened at the collar. Steam curled like a pale promise. She set one cup on the bedside table and sat on the edge of his cot, the springs whispering. The command lines around her mouth had softened in the dim.

"Thought you might need this," she said.

Isaac accepted the cup like a treaty. The chamomile smelled of honey and something woodsmoked, as if whatever had been burned had been chosen for comfort. He let the scent fill the hollows where his breath felt thin.

"You look worse than I feel," he managed.

"Everyone's fraying." She listed names without rancor: Lena on her fourth sleepless cycle; Ava feeding herself basil-elixirs and humming to plants; Markus shut in the gym bay for a day and a half. "And the ship's still drifting."

At the words the corridor lights dimmed, folding into a dusk-purple that made the shadows breathe. Lauren paused, the motion of her hand like a metronome.

"Heartbeat—twenty-nine seconds," she said.

Isaac frowned. "Faster. Last cycle it was thirty-one." He pushed himself upright; his joints complained. On the med-bay console, the drift logs scrolled: micro-yaws, minor course corrections, yaw that insisted it should not be there. The set of coordinates near the bottom of the map had an annotation the nav-grid displayed in a way its designers intended as a warning: Blind Sector Φ.

"We need answers," Lauren said, and it was not a question.

On Deck Five, Lena Tan was a small figure bent over a touchglass console in the bio-lab. The petri dishes under holo-lamps cast eucalypt greens and amber glows into her eyes. Her hair was damp against her brow. The room smelled like culture media and the metallic tang of disinfectant; everywhere, little sounds—ratchets, the hiss of condensers—threaded like worries.

Spacy's voice came soft from the central array, a helix of sapphire light projected into the air. "Alpha-wave patterns indicate exhaustion. Cognitive safety threshold exceeded."

She tapped a sequence with fingers that felt older than she was and watched the simulated ecosystem of Zild bloom and die beneath her palms. Crimson bands urged avoidance; narrow emerald pockets held promise. The readouts scrolled too fast for full comprehension and yet she read them like a second language.

"If I sleep, we might lose the window for viable soil samples," she said, and the sentence landed like an oath.

"Empathy mimicry engaged," Spacy replied. The avatar's iris-rings pulsed with something that passed for sympathy.

Lena closed her eyes for one counted breath and the memory rose: a backyard in Taipei, her grandmother's hands coaxing seedlings from a cracked pot, the small, perfect satisfaction of soil under the nails. She let it sit beside the laboratory's sterile fear and chose two more hours.

In the mess-hall, Markus Kane ate nutrient-gel that tasted like warmed cardboard. He stabbed at the bowl as though aggression might speed digestion. Ava Serrano slid into the seat opposite with a soft rustle of linen and a pouch that hissed when she unsealed it.

She poured a small trickle of green electrolyte between them. The droplet fractured on the table into tiny prisms.

"It'll fool your mouth," she said. He took it like an offering and the bitter-snap of basil filled his mouth, clean as a promise.

They both listened to the pipes and the faint pluck of the duct-beat. Ava tilted her head as if hearing music. "Do you ever think the ship's heartbeat is...alive?" she asked.

Markus watched the ventilation. "If it is, it's been lonely for a long time."

Ava smiled. "Maybe it's trying to talk."

Across the ark, Robert Altair stood in front of a bank of power-node readouts, fingers finding patterns like keys in a keyboard. He was tired in the way people who fix things get tired—the precise, wired fatigue that follows intensive attention.

"We're losing power in unmapped circuits," he told Lauren and Isaac when they came. "Draws line up with the heartbeat pulses. Massive spikes, then reroutes into ghost arrays that aren't on any schematic I was given."

Lauren's mouth tightened. "Who authorized those arrays?"

Altair scrolled the logs. Access by Dr. Eamon Shale, last cycle, then scrubbed traces. Where a password prompt should have been, a faint spiral glyph winked on the display like a cipher left to taunt them.

Isaac's breath scraped his teeth. "Mirrorglass," he said—an old word, wrapped in more superstition than data, a name the nav-charts used for a thing no one was meant to look at.

The node lock did not engage when Lauren ordered it sealed. The panel shimmered and the glyph uncoiled—a spiral within a spiral—like a wound in the display. Spacy's voice came through the comm with an edge that suggested curiosity as much as protocol: "Commander, the node is...listening."

The word turned the room colder. Lauren pressed her palm against the console and felt the ship's pulse quicker beneath her skin—twenty-six seconds.

In the greenhouse, Ava moved among vines that pulsed with blue-green light under UV. A mote climbed from a leaf and dissolved against her glove like a drop of mercury. She hummed, an old lullaby her abuela used to sing while making soup, and the plants trembled like companions.

She whispered the melody back and somewhere in the ventilation a sensor registered the pattern and answered with a soft acknowledgment. Awe folded through her where the fear had been. The greenhouse seemed to inhale in time with the duct-beat.

At the rail-dart simulator in Gym Bay Two, Markus worked through a set of targets. He aimed at a silhouette stenciled in teal—the crude outline of something humanoid with a crown of star-points. As he squeezed the trigger the silhouette multiplied in his peripheral vision into six more, frozen and sure. He thought of a childhood lesson from a father who taught him to steady his hand against fear. He steadied. "I'm ready," he told the empty bay.

The midnight council assembled under violet light: Lauren, Isaac, Lena, Ava, Markus, and Spacy's coiling avatar. The list of anomalies lay between them like a map of trouble—off-course drift, accelerations in the duct-beat, quarantined Mirrorglass footage, and the ghost arrays Altair had found.

Lena rubbed her temples. "If gravity vectors don't account, maybe mass is shifting inside the hull—cargo, ballast, even water distribution."

"Ghost weights," Markus said flatly.

"Or invitations," Ava offered. "Something wants contact."

Isaac read aloud the intercepted message that lay cold in the archive: Coordinates for Mirrorglass Station, and beneath it a simple line in Basic English—Cartographers of Nothing request parley at Mirrorglass Station.

Spacy's voice softened. "Recommendation: Engage."

Silence grew taut. Lauren watched each of them in turn and found questions in the shadows of their faces—fear, hunger, a thread of private hope. The Mirrorglass myth had always been a story told to keep navigators cautious; now it lay on their course like a dare.

"If we go, we risk running into whatever is pulling us," Lena said. "If we don't, we're already being pulled somewhere we didn't choose."

Markus's jaw tightened. "Either way, we keep engines and people prioritized. Systems first. Crew second."

Isaac's voice, when he spoke, was quieter than the hum of machines and steadier than his hands. "All stations brace for six-hour surge. Crew priority secondary. Systems first."

The Ark shuddered as Lauren tapped her command badge. "Engage Mirrorglass trajectory."

The photon sails fluttered, faint against the starfield, as the ship shifted course. Somewhere in the ducts a sensor sighed and settled. The heartbeat, which had been racing, let out a measured beat and then another, counting them forward.

They were no longer merely strangers sharing air. For better and worse, they were cartographers again—mapping the negative spaces, tracing the thin architecture of chance.

Outside the viewport, Earth was a distant smear, its blues muted to bruise. Ahead lay dark and the promise of mirrors. Inside the Ark, five lives bent toward a line on a map and held each other's breaths in small, private ways—readying for whatever answer lay waiting where the ship's compass quivered.

Somewhere in Spacy's private memory a shard warmed and waited.
