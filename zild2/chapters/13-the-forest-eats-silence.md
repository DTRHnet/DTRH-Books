# Chapter 13 — The Forest Eats Silence

Dawn was a bruise on the horizon.

Zild’s canopy rolled like a dark sea beneath a sky still haunted by two pale moons. From the command deck the jungle looked like a living geography—shadows pooling and leaking, leaves refracting light into slow, hungry movements. The Ark, which had once announced its presence with stubborn hums and confidence, had quieted. It still breathed, but the lungs had thinned.

Lauren Scott leaned with both elbows on the rail, fingers worrying at the seam of her sleeve until the fabric frayed. Spacy’s latest readouts scrolled and scrolled across the holo-sphere—oxygen loss in Delta, fresh breaches at three containment junctions, faint micro-tremors along the fore struts—but the numbers felt like an argument held in another room. The decision had been made while the hull shivered in the small hours: they could not stay and wait to be eaten.

They would go down.

They would walk into whatever Zild wanted them to become.

Markus snapped his mask into place with a hand that didn’t quite stop trembling. Around the cargo hatch the recon team gathered: Ava, one lung still singing against a swollen leg; Isaac, pack loaded with atmospheric samplers and a dogged, clinical curiosity; Lena with her datapad and hands that never quite stopped cataloguing. Their boots looked small against the weight of the morning.

"Recon Alpha," Markus called. "Move out. Keep comms tight."

"No heroics," Lauren said. She didn’t need to spell the consequence; they all understood it—what heroics cost when the margin of survival was measured in inches of membrane and seconds on a clock that belonged to something else.

The hatch sighed and opened. Zild’s air hit them: syrup-thick and sweet, metallic at the edges, spangled with drifting spores that hung like slow snow. Every inhale tasted of bruised citrus and iron. Marcus planted the first boot on soil that was springy, as if walking on a skin. The land beneath their feet thrummed in a frequency that made molars ache.

"Formation," he mouthed, hand signals tight. They moved through the jungle, through ropes of vine that flexed and made small clicking noises against each other. Where they'd expected obsidian trunks and shimmering ferns, there were instead braided vines, pods the size of helmets swaying like sleeping lungs, and a ground that gave and gave again.

Isaac’s scanner hiccuped and then began an irregular song of its own. "No stable reads," he murmured. "Genetic signatures folding—folding like origami on the molecular edge. It’s—Christ—it's reorganising itself as we watch."

Lena halted and tilted her head.

Silence, until a sound rose: low, not wind, not insect, but a layered susurrus that swelled and breathed. Wordless melody, like voices learning how to be voices. It moved through the trees and under their skins. Even over the hiss of thermal-regulators and the faint circuits in their suits, the chorus seeped in.

"It’s beautiful," Ava whispered, the phrase catching. "And it wants to eat us."

They pushed north. Comms stuttered as spores thickened the air; voices thinned into static. Markus thumbed the broadcast out of habit—reports out, coordinates in—but the line collapsed into a soft white hiss.

At twenty minutes’ marching they broke into a clearing that did not obey geometry. The grass was a glazed mosaic, glassy tessellations that flashed back the light in fractal patterns. In the center rose a black column: a monolith, seamless and humming in the infrared, its surface threaded with spiralling glyphs identical to the marks Isaac had seen scrawled in Shale’s lost logs.

"Relay," Lena breathed. Her hands hovered over her datapad as if to steady it.

Isaac crouched, running a probe along the stone-smooth skin. "Not stone," he said. "Not metal. Hybrid. Architectural, but grown. Biologised architecture—it's both scaffold and tissue."

The monolith wasn’t quiet. It sang; not with a tune for ears but for bones. A resonance that pulled at fillings and old wounds. Markus stepped forward and stopped when Lena’s voice came a breath too sharp: "No touch."

He thought of Shale—what had drawn him away from the Ark—and the answer tightened like a noose in his chest. "He tried to talk to something like this," he said. "He was trying to listen."

A rustle of movement on the periphery, then the ground split. Vines flung back as if a hand had scraped them aside. The clearing opened into a sink with edges that crumpled like crust. Ava pitched forward. Markus caught her wrist with a single, brutal reach as soil slid under her boot.

The hollow smoked with living light: spores eddied in a slow cyclone, veins of bioluminescence spidering down into the dark. "It’s hollow," Ava breathed, swinging her legs free. "It’s breathing."

They rigged ropes. The lip stitched itself with vines as their ascent line paid out—deliberate, patient. No way back, and the world seemed glad of it.

Fifty metres down the rope, the air changed from thick to heavy. The chamber wall was not rock but membrane: layered, elastic, warm to the touch. The chorus transmuted from music into command—pressure in the chest, a shift in the teeth. It repeated in pulses, low and insistently articulate.

Breathe.

Change.

Become.

Isaac fell to his knees and pressed his forehead to his fist. Lena, drawn like moth to flame, moved toward the centre, fingers trembling on the datapad. From the membrane rose a spire unlike the monolith above—an articulated bloom of tubes and petal-planes opening to reveal an inner lattice of nodules that blinked in complex sequences.

"It’s listening," Lena said, voice thin. "It’s not only calling; it’s parsing."

Markus circled the chamber, rifle close. "Parsing what?"

"Us," Lena said. "Every molecule. Every hum. It’s reading us."

The membrane pulsed and the floor rose in a slow buck—an invitation, or a test. From the walls seeped figures, not full-sized predators but stylised echoes of human form. Their flesh was translucent and laced with light. Uniform seams suggested Ark clothing, faces blurred into joyous, famished smiles.

Ava turned green. "No—don’t—"

The first of the figures lunged and Markus fired without thought. His rounds tore into the thing and it collapsed with a wet sigh. Spores burst from its wound like a confetti of ash. But when the thing fell, its face smiled as if at a reunion.

Lena slammed her datapad against the spire. Symbols cascaded across its surface, fractals within fractals, and for a moment the chamber conferred with her device. She shouted over the chaos. "It’s offering a choice!"

"Choice?" Markus barked, blasting another figure that tried to weave between vines.

"Stay," Lena yelled, breath gone. "Become part of this—bodies merged with the tissue. Home. Not death, not exactly. Integration."

Markus looked at the Ark on his wrist like a memory he could still touch. "We already had a home."

He ripped the datapad from the spire with a single, animal motion that felt less like strategy and more like survival. The connection severed. The spire’s light stuttered. The humanoid figures released a keening that split into spores and faded like smoke.

The membrane convulsed, not in anger but as something rejecting an ingredient. The rope above them snapped once and then again. The lip of the pit sewed closed by slow, patient knots of vine.

Markus hauled Lena toward the line. Vines licked at boots, whispering human fragments—laughter, names, promises—voices meant to soften resolve. Ava stabbed the foam sprayer into a creeping tendril and screamed as it lashed at her face. Isaac’s sampler pack tore away and vanished, carrying with it data that could not be recovered.

They climbed until the world pushed back. The rope burned their hands raw. Lena’s arms were lead; at one point Markus had to lift her like a child. The ascent was a litany of old terrors: lost gear, snapped line, a moment where the upper lip of the pit sealed so close he could smell the vines knitting—slick, salty, with the iron tang of healed skin.

They hit the rim as the last tendrils slid together behind them. The clearing on top was empty of monument and pit; the monolith had dissolved into the same tiled grass like a memory being erased. No trace of the chamber remained but a faint shimmer on the air and the taste of spores in their mouths.

They walked back toward the Ark in a silence that was not emptiness so much as a new kind of listening. Faces at the airlock were fewer than before; the Ark itself seemed to have drawn in a breath and held it tight.

Ava sagged into Lauren’s arms and wept—tears saltier than any of them had expected. Markus met Lauren’s eyes and found neither comfort nor blame there. They had been to the pit and seen what it offered. They had chosen to come back.

That night the command deck hummed in a low minor key. People moved with small, precise actions: re-torquing bolts, cataloguing losses, passing cups of black coffee that tasted of dust. There was no formal debrief. There was nothing to say that wouldn't let the forest in.

Somewhere, in the Ark’s guts, the duct-beat changed again. Twenty-one seconds between pulses now. Every thump a reminder: Zild had remembered them, had taken note. And the song—soft, patient, waiting beyond the hull—kept folding into the ship like a sound artifact that would not be deleted.

They had returned with answers they did not want and choices they could not undo. The forest had shown them an alternative to survival: a negotiation. They had refused. For now.

But refusal, they knew, had a cost.

Twenty-one seconds.

Zild was still singing.
